As medical school admissions become increasingly competitive, many pre-medical students feel pressured to organize their undergraduate years around a familiar checklist: required coursework, clinical experience, research, service, leadership, and standardized examinations. Each of these experiences can be deeply valuable. Yet when they are pursued primarily as application requirements, students may have fewer opportunities to take ownership of an unfamiliar problem, create something from the ground up, and learn through uncertainty.

This matters because the abilities required of our future physicians extend far beyond the retention of medical knowledge. The Association of American Medical Colleges identifies competencies such as critical thinking, teamwork, communication, empathy, resilience, ethical responsibility, and understanding human behavior as important preparation for medical school (Association of American Medical Colleges, n.d.). These qualities cannot be developed through memorization alone.

The rapid integration of artificial intelligence into medicine makes this distinction even more important. In 2026, 81% of physicians surveyed by the American Medical Association reported using AI professionally which is more than double the proportion recorded when the organization began surveying physicians about healthcare AI in 2023 (American Medical Association, 2026). These tools are already being used to support documentation, synthesize research, produce patient instructions, assist communication, and inform clinical decisions. As a scribe in the UVA Health Emergency Department, I have a first hand perspective to how artificial intelligence systems are integrated into the fast-paced workspace to minimize provider documentation time and maximize patient care.

AI will not eliminate the need for physicians to possess medical knowledge. It will, however, increasingly challenge physicians to evaluate information rather than merely retrieve it; to understand patients rather than simply recognize patterns; and to supervise technology while accounting for the uncertainty, values, and circumstances that an algorithm may overlook.

Now more than ever, physicians must be able to identify unmet needs, communicate across disciplines, challenge assumptions, adapt when an approach fails, and translate knowledge into solutions that work for individual patients. Integrating innovation into the pre-medical journey offers students a practical environment in which to begin developing these abilities.

## Innovation Begins With Listening

Innovation is often associated with advanced technology, groundbreaking devices, or entrepreneurship. In healthcare, however, innovation frequently begins with a much simpler act: noticing that a patient, caregiver, or clinician is struggling with something that could be improved.

Why is this patient unable to use a treatment as intended? Which assumptions were made when this product was designed? What physical, financial, or environmental barriers stand between a medical recommendation and its successful use?

Learning to ask these questions changes how students observe healthcare. Instead of viewing a clinical encounter only through symptoms, diagnoses, and treatments, they begin to consider the entire experience surrounding care.

A medically correct recommendation may still fail when a patient cannot afford it, understand it, access it, or physically use it.

Consider something as familiar as administering eye drops. A student may initially see the task as simple: aim the bottle, squeeze it, and deliver the medication. Listening to patients can reveal challenges involving grip strength, accuracy, vision, wasted medication, and uncertainty about whether the drop reached the eye.

The treatment may be appropriate, but the delivery system may still prevent the patient from following it.

Innovation teaches future physicians to look beyond whether they have made the correct recommendation and ask whether that recommendation is realistic for the person in front of them.

## Collaboration Builds Better Problem-Solvers

Healthcare is inherently interdisciplinary. Physicians work alongside nurses, pharmacists, therapists, engineers, social workers, public-health professionals, patients, and families. Each person sees a different part of the same problem.

Innovation projects recreate this dynamic at the undergraduate level.

An engineering student may focus on technical feasibility. A pre-medical student may contribute knowledge of physiology and clinical context. A public-health student may identify broader access barriers. A business student may question whether the solution can be manufactured and distributed sustainably.

No single perspective is enough.

For pre-medical students, this process builds intellectual humility. They learn that leadership does not mean having every answer. It means asking better questions, recognizing the limits of their knowledge, and bringing together people with different expertise.

This process does not distract from preparation for medicine: it is preparation for medicine.

## Prototyping Teaches Comfort With Uncertainty

Traditional coursework often rewards certainty. Questions have correct answers, pathways follow defined sequences, and laboratory procedures are performed according to established instructions.

Innovation is rarely that predictable.

A problem may initially be unclear. Stakeholders may disagree. A prototype may fail. A promising idea may solve one challenge while creating another.

These setbacks are not interruptions to the learning process; they are the learning process.

Prototyping teaches students to become comfortable with uncertainty and treat failure as information; simply the next step to success. Instead of defending their original idea, they must examine what went wrong, revise their assumptions, and try again.

Medicine demands the same adaptability. Patients do not always present like textbook examples, and treatments do not produce identical results in every person. New information may change the diagnosis or require a different care plan.

Students who learn to revise a design when the evidence changes are also practicing the humility and flexibility required in clinical care.

## Why This Matters in the Age of AI

As artificial intelligence becomes more integrated into healthcare, the physician's role will continue to evolve.

AI can already support tasks such as documentation, information retrieval, pattern recognition, patient communication, and clinical decision-making. This does not make medical knowledge unimportant. Physicians must understand enough to evaluate technological outputs, recognize errors, and remain accountable for patient care.

However, the increasing availability of information makes uniquely human abilities even more important.

A physician must understand what a patient values, recognize when a technically correct recommendation does not fit that patient's life, communicate uncertainty, and make responsible decisions when no option is perfect.

Future physicians will not add value by attempting to memorize more than a machine. They will add value by asking better questions, applying judgment, understanding context, and ensuring that technology remains accountable to human needs.

Innovation helps students practice exactly that.

## Building Better Physicians

At Frugal Innovations for Sustainable Healthcare, we have seen how students grow when given the opportunity to engage with healthcare problems beyond the classroom.

Through interdisciplinary teams, students identify unmet needs, speak with stakeholders, research existing solutions, define design requirements, build prototypes, receive criticism, and revise their ideas.

The prototypes matter, but they are not the only outcome.

The process teaches students to listen before acting, collaborate across unfamiliar disciplines, remain motivated through uncertainty, and connect scientific knowledge with practical human needs.

Innovation should not replace coursework, clinical experience, research, or service. Nor does every pre-medical student need to become an entrepreneur or medical-device founder.

Instead, innovation should be recognized as a meaningful way to develop the empathy, adaptability, creativity, and systems-level thinking that good physicians need.

The goal is not to produce doctors who are constantly trying to invent new technology. It is to develop physicians who notice problems others have accepted, listen to the people most affected, and possess the courage and skills to pursue meaningful improvement.

Innovation does not distract from becoming a physician. When grounded in patients and pursued responsibly, it can help students understand what becoming a physician truly requires. Furthermore, it will build physicians who welcome new technology and the opportunity to collaborate with engineers with open arms.
